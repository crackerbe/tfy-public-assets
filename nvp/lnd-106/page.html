Driving after dark in prescription glasses has gotten brutal over the past few years.

Modern LED and HID headlights are up to 6x brighter than the old halogen bulbs. Oncoming beams throw harsh blue-white light across the windshield and off wet roads.

For drivers who wear glasses, that light hits even harder.

In a March 2026 AAA survey, headlight glare was a problem for 70% of drivers who wear prescription glasses, compared to 56% of drivers without glasses.

Ask an optician for help, and the usual answer is a second pair of prescription glasses just for driving.

The problem is the price tag.

The Real Price of a Second Pair Just for Night Driving

An optical shop can charge $90, $150, even $300 for a second pair just for night driving.

One driver put it this way before paying for special lenses:

“They're rather expensive but it will be worth it if it means I can drive at night again.”

A driver on special night-driving lenses

That is a lot of money for glasses that sit in the glove box most of the week.

The Failed Fixes: Why Cheap Clip-Ons Fall Flat

To dodge the $300 bill, most people try the cheap fix first: yellow clip-ons or generic "night driving" glasses from a drugstore rack.

Most run into the same three problems:

1. Clip-Ons Slip Crooked

Clip-ons hang off the front of the frame. Every bump in the road can knock them crooked.

2. Clip-Ons Leave the Sides Wide Open

Clip-ons only cover the front of the lenses.

When a tall truck pulls up behind you, its LED beams bounce off the side mirrors and pour right past the open edges of your frames.

3. Plain Yellow Tint Just Changes the Color

Yellow tint was made for old halogen bulbs. Against today's blue-white LEDs, plain tint does not block the scattered light.

It simply turns blinding white glare into blinding yellow glare.

So drivers end up stuck between two bad options:

  • Spend hundreds on a second prescription pair
  • Squint through cheap clip-ons that slip and leak glare

There is a third option: polarized amber fit-overs.
